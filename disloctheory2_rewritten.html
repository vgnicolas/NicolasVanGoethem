Research Area

From Dislocations to Incompatibility

A distributional approach to crystalline defects, from isolated dislocations and evolving line networks to diffuse incompatibility and intrinsic continuum models.

This research programme develops a unified mathematical description of crystalline defects across several length scales. Its central idea is that dislocations should be treated as intrinsically singular geometric objects and described through distributions, measures, currents and differential constraints, rather than as perturbations of globally smooth displacement fields.

The same distributional viewpoint provides a natural passage from isolated defects to distributed dislocation densities and, ultimately, to incompatible strain fields. Incompatibility then becomes the continuum-scale trace of the underlying defect structure and the natural bridge between dislocation theory, intrinsic elasticity and incompatibility-based models of plasticity.

Isolated Dislocations and Distributional Geometry

Classical elasticity is formulated in terms of smooth, single-valued displacement fields. A crystal containing a Volterra dislocation does not admit such a global description: the displacement may be multivalued, while elastic strain, rotation and defect measures remain meaningful away from the dislocation core.

The distributional formulation avoids artificial branch cuts and represents Burgers vectors, dislocation densities and compatibility defects as intrinsic generalized quantities. Singular measures and distributional differential operators encode the geometry of the defect directly, allowing isolated dislocations to be incorporated into continuum mechanics without suppressing their topological character.

Dislocation Lines, Currents and Variational Evolution

In three dimensions, dislocations form oriented curves that may bend, move, interact and organize into complex networks. Geometric measure theory provides the appropriate framework for describing such singular structures.

In collaboration with R. Scala, I developed variational models in which dislocation lines are represented by Cartesian currents. Currents retain orientation, multiplicity, Burgers-vector information and boundary constraints, while remaining stable under weak convergence and singular limits.

This framework leads to quasistatic evolution models based on incremental minimization. At each step, the crystal selects a new configuration by balancing stored elastic energy with the dissipation associated with dislocation motion. Related work addresses the Peach–Koehler force, constraint reactions, front migration and the evolution of dislocation networks.

Topological Singularities and Weak Jacobians

The distributional treatment of defects is not limited to dislocation currents. With L. De Luca and R. Scala, I introduced a weak notion of Jacobian for maps of bounded variation. This construction captures topological singularities in discontinuous fields by combining distributional derivatives, currents and geometric measure theory.

These results provide a broader analytical language for concentrated defects and singular structures, and open a route toward compactness, relaxation and homogenization problems involving large populations of defects.

From Dislocation Density to Strain Incompatibility

At the mesoscopic scale, it is no longer appropriate to resolve every dislocation line. Their collective effect is encoded by distributed defect densities and incompatible elastic strain fields.

Kröner’s relation identifies strain incompatibility with the curl of the dislocation-density tensor. In this way, the incompatibility operator transforms the singular geometry of dislocations into an intrinsic continuum descriptor. Compatible strains remain generated by displacement fields, whereas the incompatible component records the part of the strain that cannot be eliminated by a change of displacement.

This viewpoint led to work on intrinsic elasticity, the Frank tensor, Kröner’s formula and compatible–incompatible decompositions of symmetric tensor fields. It also clarifies how isolated dislocations, diffuse defect densities and incompatible strains belong to a single mathematical structure.

Towards Incompatibility-Based Continuum Mechanics

The long-term objective is to formulate continuum models in which incompatibility is not merely a derived quantity but a primary descriptor of irreversible deformation. Rather than introducing a plastic strain without directly encoding its defect content, the model isolates the canonical incompatible part of the strain and uses it as the internal variable governing the evolution.

This programme connects distributional defect theory with current work on intrinsic elasticity, higher-order models and incompatibility-governed elastoplasticity. It provides a conceptual progression from singular dislocation lines to diffuse incompatibility and, finally, to new continuum theories for solids with defects.
