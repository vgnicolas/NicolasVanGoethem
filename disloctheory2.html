Defects, Dislocations and Topological Singularities

This research programme has developed over nearly two decades, from my doctoral work on distributional models of dislocations to recent variational and geometric approaches to defect evolution and incompatibility-based continuum theories.

One of the starting points of this research dates back to my doctoral work, during which I developed a distributional description of two-dimensional Volterra dislocations. This approach was strongly influenced by the pioneering ideas of the theoretical physicists Ekkehart Kröner and Hagen Kleinert, whose geometric and field-theoretic descriptions of crystal defects helped shape the modern continuum theory of dislocations.

This work combines continuum mechanics, geometric measure theory, partial differential equations and the calculus of variations. Dislocations are treated not merely as perturbations of a smooth deformation, but as intrinsically singular objects carrying geometric and topological information.

A Distributional Approach to Dislocations

One of the starting points of this research was the development of a distributional description of two-dimensional Volterra dislocations. Classical elasticity is formulated in terms of smooth displacement fields, but such fields cannot globally represent a crystal containing dislocations: the displacement may be multivalued, while the elastic strain remains meaningful away from the defect set.

The distributional formulation avoids introducing artificial branch cuts and describes defects directly through singular measures and differential operators. The Burgers vector and the dislocation density appear as intrinsic distributional quantities, while compatibility conditions are replaced by equations involving curl and incompatibility.

This approach provided a conceptual bridge between the geometry of isolated defects and continuum models with distributed dislocation densities. It also led naturally to later work on intrinsic elasticity, the incompatibility operator and incompatibility-based models of elasto-plasticity.

These ideas now form one of the mathematical foundations of my current research on incompatibility-based elasticity and gradient theories of elasto-plasticity.

Currents, Dislocation Lines and Variational Evolution

In three dimensions, dislocations form oriented curves that may move, interact and generate networks. Their geometry is naturally represented by currents, which retain orientation, multiplicity and boundary information while allowing weak convergence and singular limits.

This line of research originated from a seminal paper by Stefan Müller and Mariapia Palombaro, which proposed a variational formulation of dislocations based on currents. Motivated by this work, Riccardo Scala initiated a systematic investigation of the approach, which eventually became the subject of his PhD thesis at SISSA during our collaboration.

Together, we developed a variational framework in which dislocation configurations are encoded by Cartesian currents, geometric measure-theoretic objects that generalize oriented curves while preserving their topology, orientation and boundary. They provide a natural weak representation of dislocation lines and Burgers vectors, making it possible to study the evolution of complex dislocation networks within a rigorous variational setting.

This leads to quasistatic evolution models based on incremental minimization. At each stage, the crystal selects a new configuration by balancing stored elastic energy against the cost of moving the dislocations. The resulting framework establishes rigorous existence results while preserving the geometric structure and conservation laws associated with dislocation lines.

Related work investigates the Peach–Koehler force, constraint reactions in dislocation networks, front migration, and phase-field-type dynamics for the dislocation strain. More broadly, this variational perspective demonstrates how geometric measure theory and continuum mechanics can be combined to describe the evolution of crystalline defects within a mathematically rigorous framework.

Jacobians and Topological Singularities

A more recent direction concerns topological singularities in maps of bounded variation. Classical Jacobian determinants are well suited to smooth or Sobolev maps, but they do not directly capture the singular structure of discontinuous fields arising in defect theory.

In collaboration with L. De Luca and R. Scala, I introduced a weak notion of Jacobian for BV maps. The construction combines distributional derivatives, currents and geometric measure theory to identify topological singularities even when the underlying map has jumps or other singular components.

The resulting framework extends the distributional philosophy developed for dislocations: defects are encoded by intrinsic measures rather than by globally smooth fields. It provides a rigorous tool for studying vortices, defects in ordered media and singular structures whose topology survives weak convergence.

Geometry, Incompatibility and Defective Crystals

Dislocations have a natural interpretation in non-Riemannian geometry. At the continuum level, they are associated with torsion, while strain incompatibility is related to the linearized Riemann curvature tensor. A defective crystal therefore cannot, in general, be described by a globally compatible Euclidean reference configuration.

My work explores this geometric viewpoint through intrinsic formulations of elasticity, the Frank tensor, Kröner's incompatibility formula and compatible–incompatible decompositions of tensor fields. These results clarify how defect densities generate incompatible elastic strains and how the geometry of the crystal is encoded in observable continuum fields.

This line of research connects directly with my current work on higher-order incompatibility-based elasticity and elasto-plasticity, where incompatibility is promoted from a compatibility constraint to a constitutive state variable.

From Discrete Defects to Continuum Plasticity

A central long-term problem is the derivation of effective continuum theories from large systems of interacting dislocations. When the number of defects increases and their characteristic scale decreases, dislocation measures may develop oscillations, concentrations and complex network structures.

The mathematical challenge is to identify the effective energy and dissipation produced by these clusters and to determine under which scaling regimes they lead to macroscopic plastic strain, hardening or strain-gradient effects. This requires combining compactness, relaxation, Γ-convergence and homogenization with the geometric constraints carried by the dislocation currents.

The theory of weak Jacobians and singular currents offers a possible route toward this objective by providing stable representations of topological charge under weak convergence. The broader aim is to connect individual defects, collective defect dynamics and predictive continuum models of plasticity within a single variational framework.
